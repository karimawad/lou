If you're a US citizen or green card holder, a US tax filing obligation exists regardless of where you live. Moving to Canada does not change that.

This surprises a lot of people. Filing Canadian taxes makes sense. What is less obvious is that the US runs a parallel system: citizenship-based taxation.

Part I

How citizenship-based taxation works#

Most countries tax residents. The US taxes citizens.

A US citizen living in Toronto, Vancouver, or Calgary files a Form 1040 each year. All income, including income earned in Canada and reported on a T4, goes on that return. Even when no US tax is owed.

The US-Canada tax treaty is what prevents double taxation. Canadian taxes paid offset any US liability through the foreign tax credit. For most Americans in Canada with employment income, the US tax bill after credits works out to zero or close to it.

But a zero-tax return is still a return.

Part II

What goes on a US return#

Canadian income needs to be converted from CAD to USD at the IRS annual average exchange rate for that tax year. Not the current rate. Not the rate you used at your bank. The IRS publishes the annual average rate each year.

Employment income from a T4 goes on Form 1040. Investment income from T3 and T5 slips goes on Schedule B. Capital gains from T5008 go on Schedule D and Form 8949. Foreign taxes paid go on Form 1116, the foreign tax credit form.

Part III

Canadian accounts and US disclosure requirements#

Accounts held in Canada may trigger US disclosure requirements separate from the tax return.

The FBAR (FinCEN 114) covers foreign financial accounts that exceeded $10,000 USD in aggregate at any point during the year. Form 8938 covers higher thresholds under FATCA.

RRSPs can be deferred under the US-Canada treaty with a Form 8833 election. TFSAs are treated as foreign grantor trusts and reported on Form 3520 and Form 3520-A. Canadian mutual funds and ETFs held outside registered accounts may be PFICs (Passive Foreign Investment Companies) and reported on Form 8621.

Part IV

What about people who have never filed#

Many Americans in Canada discover this obligation late. That is not unusual.

The IRS has a formal catch-up path: the Streamlined Foreign Offshore Procedures. Non-willful non-filers can submit three years of returns and six years of FBARs, with penalties waived.
